A Field Guide to the Performative Male Reader

We catalogue our rarest animals. Here’s the full taxonomy.

by Malory · 12 June 2026

Recently, the internet looked up from its phone, noticed a man reading a book in public, and decided someone must’ve been running a scam.

Not about anything important, you understand. But about the book. Because there could be no way that the man with an open novel on the L train was reading that novel, so he must have been performing it. He must have been pretending to read for an audience of women he hoped would mistake him for the sensitive type and take pity on him. Because - don’t you know? - real men don’t read books, so this must all be a long con.

And, as it does, the internet did its thing, and the Performative Male Reader (PMR) now boasts a host of his own trend pieces, his own advice-column cameos and his own survey literature. And now (you’re welcome, by the way) he has his own field guide.

Because that’s what we do with our rarest animals. We catalogue them.

The Airport Mirage

Lector elordii. Habitat: departure lounges and paparazzi feeds. Distinguishing marks: more books than hands.

Julianna Salguero describes this specimen of the PMR as “a replication of a blurry photo of Jacob Elordi carrying three books in one giant hand as he strolls through an airport”; he is, she concludes, “a mirage”. This male reader is a cryptid. He is photographed at a distance, his evidence for existence is contested and the likelihood for him being real is inferred. These men who claim to have read every Cormac McCarthy novel twice have the real-world standing of the Loch Ness Monster, just with patchier photographic coverage.

The Accessory

Lector ornamentalis. Habitat: coffee shops with exposed brick. Distinguishing marks: Infinite Jest, title out, bookmark unmoved since March.

Sadly, the moment for this version of the PMR has now passed, as the Infinite-Jest-as-prop economy peaked in 2025. There was a brief moment when a thousand-page Wallace hardback was briefly a viable substitute for a personality, but that is winding down with the costume still circulating after the party. Mind you, he was always rare (a thousand pages is a lot of prop for one flat white) but he has poisoned the watering hole on his way out and the survivors get tested for his disease. He is survived by his tote bag.

The Suspect in the Wild

Lector suspectus. Habitat: your relationship. Distinguishing marks: reads, which is the problem.

Dazed ran an advice column titled “Help! My boyfriend doesn’t read”, which treats the non-reading boyfriend as a manageable chronic condition, somewhere between a snorer and a vegan, whilst at the same time stating “men who read now feel like such an anomaly that spotting one in the wild is cause for suspicion.”

You have to admire that. The boyfriend who doesn’t read is survivable, but the boyfriend who does read has to have something wrong with him. Apparently, a man can clear the bar by lying on the sofa and fail it by reading Middlemarch on the sofa, at which point his partner is invited to wonder what he’s up to. (Reading Middlemarch, mostly. It’s very long.)

Just as a side note, the sociologist Laura Scholes has spent twenty years interviewing children about reading, and finds this suspicious approach to boys reading starts early. Reading for young men becomes “a criterion for benchmarking or demarcating ‘uncool’ students with a boy’s commitment to reading and schoolwork challenging his masculinity”.

The Press Release

Lector curatus. Habitat: lifestyle verticals. Distinguishing marks: recommends books ‘other guys should check out’.

InsideHook now runs a monthly series in which men in literary spaces recommend books to other men. Now, I’ve got nothing unkind to say about men recommending books to men; I’d like fifty times more of it. But that male reading has become sufficiently non-spontaneous that it needs an editorial frame, the way wolf populations need a reintroduction programme, isn’t great, zoologically speaking.

This version of the PMR is not reading, exactly. He’s being released into the wild, with tags on, so the population can be monitored.

The Refusenik

Lector negans. Habitat: other people’s survey forms. Distinguishing marks: declines to be perceived.

Elle jones asked actual men, via an actual survey, under the wonderful title “should men be allowed to read things?”, what was going on here. One respondent replied: “If you’re going to use this form to write an article about performative males and how they read books for show (and how they’re portrayed negatively), I don’t think it’s a real problem”.

This clearly shows that this PMR is the healthiest animal in this guide. Offered the chance to be observed, classified and written up, he looked at the clipboard, looked at the researcher, and declined the premise. Magnificent. The Refusenik alone has noticed that the taxonomy is the predator.

The Clandestine Reader

Lector clandestinus. Habitat: unknown, by design. Distinguishing marks: none visible. That is the point.

This is the one species that all of this current chat has never actually photographed. In 2015 Scholes isolated a cluster of Queensland schoolchildren she called the Clandestine Readers: sixty children who loved reading while perceiving a peer culture hostile to it. Forty-three were boys. “While they enjoyed reading,” she writes, “they felt compelled to conceal their endeavours.”

Now read that against everything above. Because the best-documented male reader is not a man performing reading he isn’t doing. He is a boy concealing the reading he is doing. The online frenzy has the PMR fraud running in exactly the wrong direction, and has built a small content economy on the error: frisking the man with the hardcover for showy fakery while the reading population files past with its books out of sight, having learned at school what happens to the visible boy with a book.

Look, we catalogue what’s rare, and for all me being snarky above, the sums here are brutal. The NEA has American men’s fiction reading at 35.1 per cent in 2012, 33 in 2017 and 27.7 in 2022. The time-use data is worse, with daily reading for pleasure roughly halving in twenty years, and the population splitting into a small remnant who read more hours than ever and a large majority who read none.

Men are packed into the second group.

So, no, of course the reading man didn’t become performative. Rather, he became scarce, and scarcity does to credibility what it does to everything else and now a man reading on the train feels like CGI because, statistically, he nearly is.

But all of this internet frenzy about who is and who isn’t reading isn’t new, though. The last time it happened, women were the hysterical focus. The historian Kate Flint documented that between 1837 and 1914, “the awareness of Victorians and Edwardians of that discrete category, ‘the woman reader’, and the hypotheses about her special characteristics, as well as her presumed needs and interests, affected the composition, distribution, and marketing of literature.” Doctors worried about her. Advice manuals managed her. Reviewers savaged the sensation novels she liked as fiction “which deliberately catered to compulsive forms of consumption”, books not read but “devoured by women”. Whole novels were written about the dangers of women reading novels, which at least kept the supply chain moving.

Now, swap the genders. Swap Lady Audley’s Secret for a Kindle full of LitRPG and the whole thing reassembles. Suspicious reader? Check. A suspect genre? Check? Compulsive consumption? Check? Columnists fretting at scale in think-pieces? Vheckity check check.

All of this leaves the blind spot, and the blind spot, as they most often are, is the stupid part. This focus on the PMR counts what it can photograph and gets exited about hardcovers on trains, spines in coffee shops and three books in one giant hand. But the reading men of 2026 are holding a Kindle, or a phone, on which a 700-page pulp tome is indistinguishable at surveillance distance from email.

The visible reader gets accused of faking it; but the invisible one doesn’t register at all because Scholes’s clandestine ten-year-olds grew up, kept the camouflage, and all that camouflage now works so well the culture has ruled the species extinct apart from some fakes. That sort of blindness doesn’t just happen. You have to commit to not bloody looking.

The Seventh Species

Quis custodiet ipsos lectores. Habitat: everywhere above this line.

There’s one more specimen, and you’ve already spotted them. The columnist with the clipboard. In fact, the most common species in all this isn’t reading men, or fake reading men, but people writing about them: the advice columnist, the essayist, the surveyor, and now, bringing up the rear with a field guide, me.

The performative male reader has been manufactured in pieces exactly like this one, with each entry teaching one more man that opening a book in public files him under suspect, and one more boy that the lunchtime audit has a print edition waiting for him.

The Refusenik had it right. The Clandestine Readers had it right at ten.

Don’t fill in the form.

See you next Friday. Bring a book. Wear something inconspicuous.
