Men's and Boys' Reading Statistics: What the Data Actually Says

A sourced, myth-checked roundup of where men and boys stand with reading. These are the verified numbers, the zombie stats to avoid, and the point where audited data runs out and platform estimates begin.

by Malory · 14 June 2026

The story everyone tells is simple: men and boys have stopped reading.

I write fiction a largely male audience reads daily, and I research men’s and boys’ reading, so I spend a lot of my time on this. The real picture is messier than the headline, and a good deal more interesting.

Sure, the gap is real. But a lot of the figures in circulation are wrong, some are a decade out of date, and much of the “stopped reading” discourse misses both where many male readers have gone and how the decline was built. My research finds a constructed decline: less a failure of male attention than the product of how fiction was distributed, schooled and gendered, with technology only the latest culprit in a panic that recurs every generation.

I’ve cut the stats that don’t survive scrutiny (the “80% of fiction readers are women” claim is one of them) and flagged where the reliable data ends. If you’re a journalist, you can lean hard on anything in the first six sections, but treat Section 7 (the platform-migration story) as estimates.

The myths that need shooting are in Section 8.

1. The headline gap: men and fiction

The most authoritative source is the US Census Bureau’s Survey of Public Participation in the Arts (SPPA), analysed by the National Endowment for the Arts. It breaks fiction reading out by sex over a decade.

  • Fiction reading (a novel or short story in the past year), by gender: women 54.6% / men 35.1% (2012) → women 50.0% / men 33.0% (2017) → women 46.9% / men 27.7% (2022). Roughly one in four men read a novel or short story in 2022. (NEA, Jan 2025)
  • The men–women fiction gap is about 19 points and has held steady for a decade. It stayed flat (rather than widening) only because women’s fiction reading fell at a comparable rate. (NEA, Jan 2025)
  • Any-book reading by US adults fell from 54.6% (2012) to 52.7% (2017) to 48.5% (2022); fiction across all adults fell from 45.2% to 41.8% to 37.6% over the same waves. (NEA, Jan 2025)
  • 75% of US adults read a book in any format in the past year (2021), with no statistically significant difference by gender on that headline measure. The male gap shows up in fiction (above) and at the heavy-reader end (below), not in whether a man read a book at all. The biggest divide in who reads is education: 39% of adults with a high-school education or less read zero books, versus 11% of college graduates. (Pew Research Center, 2021)
  • Americans read an average of 12.6 books in 2021, the lowest Gallup has recorded since it began asking in 1990. (Gallup, Jan 2022)
  • At the high end, the gap is wide: 9% of women but only 2% of men say they read 50+ books a year (men cluster in the 1–10 range). (YouGov, 2018)

2. How the gap looks over time (and why “collapse” is too strong)

  • Literary reading by US adults (novels, stories, poetry or plays) went 56.9% (1982) → 54.0% (1992) → 46.7% (2002), then rose to 50.2% in 2008, the first increase in 26 years, among both men and women. The decline is real but uneven. (NEA, Reading at Risk, 2004; NEA, Reading on the Rise, 2009)
  • Reading for pleasure in the US declined roughly 3% a year across 2003–2023, in the most rigorous analysis of the time-use record to date. The decline ran across the whole population, men included. (Bone et al., iScience, 2025)
  • On an average day in 2023, US women spent about 19 minutes and men about 12 minutes on leisure reading, a gap of six to seven minutes, from federal time-diary data. (US Bureau of Labor Statistics, American Time Use Survey 2023)

The takeaway for anyone writing about this is that men read less fiction than women, and that’s well documented. The gap is holding steady, and the 20-year slide in reading is something men and women are doing together.

3. The boys’ story

This is where the data is most dramatic, and it comes from the standard British source: the National Literacy Trust’s Annual Literacy Survey. The figures below are the 2025 wave (fieldwork January–March 2025). The NLT’s 2026 wave has since landed and should be folded in on the next refresh.

  • In 2025, 25.7% of boys aged 8–18 said they enjoy reading in their free time, versus 39.1% of girls, a 13.4-point gap. Overall enjoyment hit 32.7%, the lowest in the survey’s 20-year history. (National Literacy Trust, Children and Young People’s Reading in 2025)
  • The collapse year was 2024: boys’ free-time reading enjoyment fell to 28.2% (from 40.5% in 2023), while girls’ fell to 40.5% (from 45.3%). The gender gap nearly tripled in a single year, from 4.8 points to 12.3 points, driven mostly by boys. (National Literacy Trust, Children and Young People’s Reading in 2024)
  • Boys’ reading enjoyment has roughly halved since its 2016 peak of 52.4%. (National Literacy Trust, 2024)
  • Teenage boys are where it’s worst. In 2024, only 21.6% of boys aged 14–16 enjoyed reading (versus 36.9% of girls), and just 11.7% read daily, about one in ten. (National Literacy Trust, 2024)
  • Daily reading is at record lows: in 2025, 15.4% of boys read daily versus 21.6% of girls (overall daily reading 18.7%, an all-time low). (National Literacy Trust, 2025)

The enjoyment gap had been narrowing, from 10.7 points in 2005 to 4.8 points in 2023, before it snapped wide open in a single year. This is a recent swing, not a permanent fact of nature.

4. Attainment: girls lead, almost everywhere

  • United States (NAEP): girls outscore boys in reading at every grade, by about 8 points at Grade 8 (boys 255, girls 263; 2022) and 13 points at Grade 12 (boys 279, girls 292; 2019). Girls have led on NAEP reading at every grade in every year since the first assessment in 1971. (NCES, Digest of Education Statistics, Table 221.10)
  • Internationally (PISA 2022): 15-year-old girls outscored boys in reading by 24 points across OECD countries, and led in every country and economy except two. (OECD, PISA 2022 Results, Vol. I)
  • At Grade 4 (PIRLS 2021): girls outscored boys in reading by 16 points on average across 43 countries, and boys did not lead in a single country. (IEA, PIRLS 2021 International Results)

5. Reading for fun is falling for children… fast

  • United States: only 14% of 13-year-olds read for fun “almost every day” in 2023, the lowest ever recorded, down 13 points from 2012. (NAEP Long-Term Trend, 2023)
  • 52% of US 13–18-year-olds say they never read for pleasure, or do so a month or less; only 24% of teens (versus 38% of tweens) say they enjoy reading “a lot.” Teen screen use rose 17% from 2019 to 2021, to 8 hours 39 minutes a day. (Common Sense Media, Census 2021)

6. What boys actually want, and often can’t find

  • 89% of children (6–17) say their favourite books are ones they picked themselves, and 88% say they’re more likely to finish a book they chose. Choice is the single biggest lever on whether a child finishes a book. (Scholastic, Kids & Family Reading Report, 2019)
  • 42% of children say they have trouble finding books they like, rising to 59% among infrequent readers. (Scholastic, 2019)
  • Comics and graphic novels gain appeal from age 8 and are nearly equally popular among frequent, moderate and infrequent readers, one of the few formats that reliably engages reluctant readers. (Scholastic, 2019)
  • UK: boys eligible for free school meals are far less likely to own a book, 67.1% versus 77.5% of other boys. Disadvantaged boys are more likely to say “reading on screen is cooler than reading a print book” (49.9% of boys who don’t enjoy reading agree). (National Literacy Trust, teenage boys & technology)

The pattern is consistent: boys read when they can choose, when the book moves, and when the format is one they already use, screens included.

7. Where the male readers, potentially, went

This is the part of the story journalists most want and the data least supports. Web-fiction and self-publishing platforms don’t publish audited demographics, so the figures below are industry bodies and third-party traffic estimates, and are labelled as such.

  • Audiobooks: US sales rose 13% in 2024 to $2.22 billion and a further 9% in 2025 to $2.43 billion; about 51% of Americans 18+ have listened to one, and general fiction is the single largest revenue genre (27%). The APA publishes no male/female listener split, so we cannot claim audiobooks “skew male.” (Audio Publishers Association, via Publishers Weekly, 2025)
  • Graphic novels are a growth-then-pullback story: unit sales fell 22.4% in 2023 and another 11.5% in 2024, to 24.4 million units, with manga a large part of the category. The “manga is booming, that’s where the boys went” line is out of date. The category peaked around 2021–22. (Circana BookScan, via Comics Beat, 2024)
  • Royal Road, the home of LitRPG and progression fantasy, draws tens of millions of visits a month from a heavily male-skewing audience; Similarweb’s modelled estimate runs around 70% male, with 18–24 the largest age band. This is a third-party traffic estimate, not audited platform data. It is, however, the best available evidence that the male-skewing genre platforms exist at meaningful scale. It is not a hard headcount. (Similarweb, royalroad.com)
  • A corrective worth keeping handy: Wattpad’s audience is predominantly young women (90m+ users). The male web-fiction audience lives on Royal Road and its neighbours, not Wattpad, and the two are routinely confused. (Wattpad corporate / aggregated)

8. The myths to retire

If you take nothing else from this page, take these. Each of the following is in heavy circulation, and each is wrong or unsupported.

  • “80% of fiction readers are women” / “only 20% are men.” No traceable survey exists. When Vox asked the data owners, Circana BookScan said they don’t break sales out by gender and that 80% “seems quite high,” and Nielsen BookData said “it has never been the case.” The line traces to a 2007 NPR story and a 1997 New York Times aside, neither citing a survey. If you need a real buying gap, use this: in 2017 UK women bought around 60% of new fiction and men a little under 40% (Nielsen BookData, reported via Vox; UK, buying not reading, pre-pandemic). (Vox, Jan 2025)
  • “Men’s literary reading fell from 57% in 1982 to 41%.” Not supported as stated. The 56.9% (1982) figure is all literature, all adults, not men specifically. That series also fell to 46.7% by 2002 before rebounding. The “41%” is almost certainly the 2017 all-adult fiction figure (41.8%) bolted onto the wrong baseline. Use the verified NEA fiction-by-gender numbers in Section 1 instead.
  • “Manga is booming, that’s where the boys are now.” That was true in 2021, but the category has declined two years running since. (Section 7.)

9. What the data does and doesn’t support

It supports: women read more fiction than men (a stable ~19-point gap, NEA); women spend modestly more time reading (ATUS); reading has declined across the whole population over 20 years (iScience); boys’ reading enjoyment in the UK collapsed in 2024–25 (NLT); and girls out-attain boys in reading in nearly every country measured (PISA, PIRLS, NAEP).

It does not support: an “80/20” fiction split; a claim that men’s reading uniquely collapsed (women’s fell at a similar rate); a claim that the adult gender gap is currently widening (it’s flat); or a current manga boom.

10. On sourcing

Every figure here is drawn from the primary source: government surveys (NEA/SPPA, NCES/NAEP, BLS/ATUS), inter-governmental assessments (OECD/PISA, IEA/PIRLS), the National Literacy Trust, peer-reviewed analysis, and named industry bodies. Nothing comes from secondary write-ups, except where a journalist (Vox) did the original fact-checking with the data owners, and I’ve credited them for it.

There are two deliberate omissions: I’ve left out the exact 2024 NAEP male/female scores (not yet in a static, citable table) and treated the American Time Use Survey minute figures as the published 2023 estimates. Where data is modelled or estimated rather than measured, chiefly Section 7, it’s labelled.
